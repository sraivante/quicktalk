30-Day Indian Raga Practice Plan

Purpose This plan helps a beginner move from basic swara practice to simple raga recognition and question-answer understanding. It is not a replacement for a guru or teacher, but it gives a practical study path.

Week 1: Voice, Sa, and Basic Swaras

Day 1 Practice Sa with a tanpura or drone for 10 minutes. Then sing Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni Sa slowly. Goal: stable pitch.

Day 2 Practice lower Sa, middle Sa, and upper Sa. Learn the meaning of mandra, madhya, and taar saptak.

Day 3
Practice shuddha notes in Bilawal style. Sing slowly, not loudly.

Day 4 Learn komal notes: komal Re, komal Ga, komal Dha, komal Ni. Compare them with shuddha notes.

Day 5
Learn tivra Ma. Compare shuddha Ma and tivra Ma.

Day 6
Revise aroha and avaroha. Say the definition aloud: aroha is ascent, avaroha is descent.

Day 7 Listen to one recording each of Yaman, Bhairav, and Bhoopali. Do not analyze too much; just notice mood.

Week 2: First Ragas

Day 8
Study Raga Yaman. Focus on tivra Ma. Sing aroha and avaroha slowly.

Day 9
Study Raga Bhoopali. Notice that it has no Ma and no Ni.

Day 10
Compare Yaman and Bhoopali. Write three differences.

Day 11
Study Raga Bhairav. Practice komal Re and komal Dha slowly with weight.

Day 12
Study Raga Kafi. Notice komal Ga and komal Ni.

Day 13
Study Raga Bhairavi. Notice the soft devotional color.

Day 14
Answer 20 questions from the Q&A file. Check answers against the book.

Week 3: Mood, Time, and Pakad

Day 15 Learn what vadi and samvadi mean. Pick five ragas and identify their vadi and samvadi from the table.

Day 16 Learn what pakad means. Try to hum the pakad-like movement of Yaman and Bhoopali from a teacher or recording.

Day 17
Study morning ragas: Bhairav, Ahir Bhairav, Jaunpuri, Asavari.

Day 18
Study evening ragas: Yaman, Hamsadhwani, Durga, Kedar.

Day 19
Study late-night ragas: Darbari Kanada, Malkauns, Bageshri, Chandrakauns.

Day 20
Study monsoon ragas: Miyan ki Malhar, Megh, Desh.

Day 21
Create 10 of your own questions from the raga table.

Week 4: Comparison and Dataset Preparation

Day 22
Compare Malkauns and Chandrakauns. Focus on Ni.

Day 23
Compare Marwa and Puriya. Focus on Pa absence and note emphasis.

Day 24
Compare Bhairav and Ahir Bhairav. Focus on komal Re and komal Ni.

Day 25
Compare Kafi and Bageshri. Focus on Pa treatment and mood.

Day 26
Practice explaining one raga in five sentences: thaat, time, mood, aroha, and avaroha.

Day 27
Convert five raga explanations into assistant-style Q&A.

Day 28 Make vocabulary Q&A for these words: aroha, avaroha, vadi, samvadi, pakad, thaat, alap, bandish, taan, meend.

Day 29
Listen to three ragas and write what you feel. Do not worry if your answer is simple.

Day 30
Revise the whole pack. Try to answer: Why is a raga more than a scale?

Daily Rule Quality is better than speed. Five minutes of correct listening is better than thirty minutes of careless singing.

Indian Raga Teaching Book in English

Purpose This book is a practical English introduction to Indian ragas for learners, singers, instrumentalists, and dataset builders. It explains the basic grammar of Hindustani raga music, gives a structured table of important ragas, and ends with question-answer material based on the same book.

Important note A raga is not just a scale. A scale tells which notes are available. A raga tells how notes behave: which notes are strong, which are weak, how phrases move, what mood is created, and what time or atmosphere suits it. The same notes can produce different ragas when their movement, stress, and characteristic phrases are different.

Svara The seven basic svaras are Sa, Re, Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha, and Ni. Sa and Pa are usually fixed reference points in Hindustani music. Re, Ga, Dha, and Ni can be shuddha or komal. Ma can be shuddha or tivra. Sa has no komal or tivra form.

Saptak A saptak is an octave. Mandra saptak is the lower octave, madhya saptak is the middle octave, and taar saptak is the upper octave. A raga normally moves across more than one saptak, but its personality may prefer a certain register.

Aroha and Avaroha Aroha is the ascending movement of a raga. Avaroha is the descending movement. They do not always contain the same notes. Some ragas are vakra, meaning their movement is zigzag instead of straight.

Vadi and Samvadi Vadi is the most important note of a raga. Samvadi is the second most important note. These notes are not merely loud notes; they are resting points and emotional centers.

Pakad Pakad is the identifying phrase of a raga. A listener may recognize a raga from its pakad even before hearing the full scale.

Chalan Chalan means the movement or pathway of the raga. It includes typical phrases, permitted turns, and the way one note approaches another.

Rasa and Bhava Rasa means aesthetic flavor and bhava means emotional feeling. A raga may sound devotional, serious, romantic, peaceful, heroic, playful, or meditative.

The thaat system is a classification method in Hindustani music. A thaat is a parent scale. It helps organize ragas, but it does not fully define them. Two ragas from the same thaat can sound completely different.

The ten commonly taught thaats are:
Bilawal: all shuddha notes.
Kalyan: uses tivra Ma.
Khamaj: associated with komal Ni in descent.
Kafi: uses komal Ga and komal Ni.
Bhairav: uses komal Re and komal Dha.
Bhairavi: uses komal Re, komal Ga, komal Dha, and komal Ni.
Asavari: commonly uses komal Ga, Dha, and Ni in descent.
Todi: uses komal Re, komal Ga, tivra Ma, and komal Dha.
Marwa: uses komal Re and tivra Ma, usually without Pa in major ragas.
Poorvi: uses komal Re, tivra Ma, and komal Dha.

The thaat is a map, not the journey. The raga is the journey.

Jati describes how many notes are used in ascent and descent.

Audav means five notes.
Shadav means six notes.
Sampurna means seven notes.

Examples: Bhoopali is audav-audav because it uses five notes in ascent and descent. Yaman is sampurna-sampurna because it normally uses seven notes both ways. Asavari may show different ascent and descent behavior depending on style.

The number of notes alone does not define the raga. Bhoopali and Deshkar may share note material, but their movement and emphasis differ.

Many Hindustani ragas are linked with a time of day. Morning ragas often create calm, devotional, or serious feelings. Afternoon ragas may feel bright, dry, or reflective. Evening ragas often introduce warmth, sweetness, or longing. Late-night ragas may be deep, grave, romantic, or meditative.

Examples:
Bhairav is a morning raga.
Multani is an afternoon raga.
Yaman is an evening raga.
Darbari Kanada and Malkauns are late-night ragas.

The time theory is a traditional guide. In modern concerts, artists sometimes perform ragas outside their time, but the traditional association remains important for study.

Alap is the slow exploration of a raga without percussion. It introduces the raga's notes, mood, and movement.

Bandish is a fixed composition. It gives a melodic and rhythmic home for the raga. A learner should study bandish because it shows how the raga breathes.

Taan is a fast melodic run. A good taan must still respect the raga's grammar. Speed without raga identity is not good music.

Meend is a glide from one note to another. Andolan is a slow oscillation around a note. Gamak is a strong oscillating movement. Kan-swar is a grace note. These ornaments are essential in many ragas.

Improvisation does not mean random singing. It means creative movement inside the discipline of the raga.

Begin with Sa. Tune your voice or instrument carefully to Sa and Pa. Sing slowly. Then practice aroha and avaroha. After that, practice the pakad. Then sing short phrases and rest on vadi and samvadi notes.

A good practice order:
Tanpura or drone.
Long Sa practice.
Slow aroha and avaroha.
Pakad.
Short alap phrases.
Bandish.
Simple taans.
Listening to masters.

Never learn ragas only as note lists. Always learn at least three things: scale, pakad, and mood.

Yaman and Bhoopali Both may feel devotional, but Yaman uses tivra Ma and all seven notes, while Bhoopali is pentatonic and avoids Ma and Ni.

Bhairav and Ahir Bhairav Bhairav uses komal Re and komal Dha with a grave morning mood. Ahir Bhairav combines Bhairav-like komal Re with a softer komal Ni color.

Malkauns and Chandrakauns Both are pentatonic and late-night ragas. Malkauns uses komal Ni, while Chandrakauns uses shuddha Ni, giving a brighter dreamy shade.

Kafi and Bageshri Both use komal Ga and komal Ni, but Bageshri has a more intimate late-night mood and treats Pa weakly or sparingly in many styles.

Marwa and Puriya Both belong to the Marwa area and avoid Pa, but Marwa creates sharp sunset tension around komal Re and tivra Ma, while Puriya gives more weight to Ni and Ga.

Desh and Khamaj Both are related to Khamaj, but Desh has a strong rainy and patriotic flavor with characteristic movement around R, M, P, and Ni.

Mistake 1: Treating a raga as only a scale. Correction: Learn pakad, chalan, vadi, samvadi, and mood.

Mistake 2: Singing too fast too early.
Correction: Begin with slow alap and stable notes.

Mistake 3: Ignoring shruti and intonation. Correction: Practice with tanpura and listen carefully to note placement.

Mistake 4: Mixing ragas accidentally. Correction: Compare similar ragas and note forbidden or weak notes.

Mistake 5: Memorizing names without sound. Correction: Hear each raga from a good recording and sing its phrases.

Hindustani and Carnatic Raga Overview

Indian classical music has two major classical systems: Hindustani music of North India and Carnatic music of South India. Both use ragas, but their teaching style, terminology, compositions, and performance structures differ.

Both systems use:
- Sa as the tonal center.
- Raga as a melodic framework.
- Tala or taal as rhythmic cycle.
- Ornamentation such as gamaka or note movement.
- Improvisation within grammar.
- Compositions as learning anchors.

Hindustani music often gives great space to slow alap, gradual raga expansion, and long improvisation. A performance may move from alap to vilambit bandish and then to faster compositions and taans. The thaat system is commonly used for classification.

Carnatic music often uses kritis, varnams, and a highly organized system of exercises. It has the melakarta system of parent scales. Gamakas are central and often define the raga more strongly than the plain scale.

Melakarta and Thaat

The Hindustani thaat system has ten common parent scales. The Carnatic melakarta system has 72 parent scales. A melakarta is a complete seven-note parent raga with systematic rules. A thaat is also a parent scale, but the practical raga behavior still depends heavily on chalan, pakad, and tradition.

Examples of Carnatic Ragas Known Widely

Mayamalavagowla Often used for beginner lessons in Carnatic music. It has a serious and balanced sound.

Shankarabharanam Similar in note material to the major scale and related to Bilawal in Hindustani thinking.

Kalyani Uses prati madhyamam, similar to tivra Ma, and is related in broad note material to Yaman/Kalyan.

Todi or Hanumatodi A major Carnatic raga with strong gamaka identity. It should not be treated as identical to Hindustani Todi.

Kharaharapriya
An important melakarta raga with a warm and expressive sound.

Charukeshi
A Carnatic-origin raga also adopted into Hindustani and light music contexts.

Hamsadhwani
A pentatonic raga used in both systems, often as an auspicious opening raga.

Abhogi
A pentatonic raga known in both systems, though treatment may differ.

The same raga name may not mean exactly the same musical behavior in both systems. For example, Todi in Hindustani and Todi in Carnatic are not identical in practical handling. Always learn from the tradition-specific grammar.

For question-answer generation, keep Hindustani and Carnatic facts separate unless the passage explicitly compares them. This prevents the model from mixing systems.
